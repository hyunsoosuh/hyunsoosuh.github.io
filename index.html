I combine structural, experimental, and quasi-experimental methods to model and estimate the effects of parental investments, school quality, and health on human capital formation, and to draw policy implications.

Job Market Paper

Who Gains from Maternal Employment? Self-Employment, Childcare, and Child Development

Abstract: Whether a mother's employment helps or harms her child depends on how well formal childcare substitutes for her time. I estimate a household life-cycle model of work arrangements, time allocation, and child skill formation on UK panel data, using the paid-versus-self-employment margin to separate flexibility from earnings level and volatility. With a young child, the wife sorts into a more flexible work arrangement while the husband's remains unchanged. The mother specializes in child-rearing, consistent with a comparative advantage in producing child skills relative to the labor market. Formal care substitutes well for a non-college mother's time but poorly for a college mother's, whose time is the child's most productive input. Full-time paid employment therefore lowers skills by 0.14 to 0.20 SD for children of college mothers and has little effect otherwise. Flexible hours without a pay penalty raise maternal employment by about 4 percentage points and raise welfare, but leave child skills unchanged. Childcare raises skills mainly for children of non-college mothers, so tagging it by education targets those who gain the most. Pairing the two policies benefits both groups and raises the projected lifetime earnings of children of non-college mothers by about 2.5%.
